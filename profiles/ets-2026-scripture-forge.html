How to read this

Each tool has two pages. The Summary page gives the short version: what the tool is, who has told us what, and where the evidence stands. The Findings & Evidence page sets out all fourteen criteria, the score each has been given so far, and what that score rests on. Read the basis column rather than the number: the number is shorthand, the basis is the finding.

Where a score comes from

Every criterion is scored 1 to 4 against wording agreed in advance, so that a 3 means the same thing for every tool. Scores are never added up or averaged, and there is no overall mark. A tool that scores 2 on something your organisation does not care about may still be the right tool for you.

agreed
Reviewers scored it independently, then compared scores and agreed the result on the record. Where they had differed, they settled it by discussion, not by averaging or a vote.
provisional
Every reviewer who scored it gave the same number, but they have not yet discussed it. None appear in this edition yet.
reviewers differ
Reviewers scored it independently and landed on different numbers. Every score given is shown; the bar on the chart shows the lowest. None appear in this edition yet.
AI-proposed
A draft score with its reasoning, prepared with AI assistance from the evidence we gathered. It is not a human judgement. None appear in this edition yet.
contested
The evidence splits, or our own wording does not settle the case. Worth reading the basis.

Where the evidence comes from

Every score carries a tag saying what it rests on. The tags are not interchangeable: what a translator reports from a live project counts for more than what a product page says, and both are printed so you can weigh them yourself.

User-reported
A translator or team using the tool in a live project told us directly, through a survey or an interview we ran.
Facilitator-reported
A facilitator, coordinator or support worker told us about the teams they serve. Close to the work, but second-hand.
Our testing
We used the tool ourselves against a fixed set of questions and recorded what happened.
Documentation
A published page, release note, policy or repository, with the date we read it.
Builder claim
The people who make the tool told us. Printed as theirs, not as established fact.
Pending
Asked for but not yet in hand. The page says what was requested and when.
N/A
The criterion does not apply — for example, explainability for a tool with no AI in it.

The three criteria we treat as decisive

Eleven of the fourteen criteria describe how well a tool does something. Three describe whether it is safe to rely on at all, and a score of 1 on any of them is worth more of your attention than a handful of 2s elsewhere. They are marked in red on the Findings & Evidence page. Nothing here removes a tool from anyone's list — that decision belongs to your organisation — but these are the three we would want answered before committing a project to a tool.

  • 1. Problem Alignment — whether the tool solves a problem translation teams actually have. A tool that is impressive but answers a question nobody asked will not survive contact with a project, however well it scores elsewhere.
  • 11. Cost & Financial Sustainability — whether a team can still afford it in three years. Adoption is not a purchase but a dependency: a tool whose funding quietly ends leaves a project stranded mid-book.
  • 12. Data Governance & Sovereignty — who holds the translation text, on what terms, and who else can see it. Unpublished Scripture is a community's own work and sometimes a safety matter, and unlike the other criteria a mistake here cannot be undone later.

The two questions we ask before a tool goes on this list

Can we assess it? That is: is it in scope, and can we find out enough about it? A tool qualifies if it serves Bible translation or related ministry and if we can gather enough information to answer the fourteen questions — from the people using it where they exist, and otherwise from documentation, from the people who built it and from our own use. A tool we cannot find out enough about is named in the wider catalogue and marked as not assessed, rather than being scored on guesswork.

Is it worth your time here? That is: among the tools we could assess, we gave priority to those that address a problem organisations in this room actually have, that more than one team has used, or that stand for a choice many of you are weighing this year. This is a decision about what fits a short session, not a ranking, and a tool being absent says nothing against it.

The fourteen criteria

Every tool is asked the same fourteen questions. ● marks the three we treat as decisive; ● marks the four that need real users before they can be answered. The full wording for each score, 1 to 4, is in the appendix at the back.

1. Problem Alignment ● — What problem does it solve?
2. Setup Complexity — How easy is it to set up?
3. Workflow Integration — Can this tool be easily integrated into the workflow?
4. User Reach & Adoption ● — How many people are using it, and who are they?
5. Ease of Use & Learning Curve — How intuitive is the tool to use daily?
6. Project Implementation ● — How many projects have implemented this tool?
7. Implementation Horizon — What can be implemented now, and what is promised later?
8. Workflow Stage Fit — Where in its domain's workflow does the tool fit, and how much of that workflow does it cover?
9. Post-Implementation Support ● — What support is available after a team adopts it?
10. Retention & Sustained Impact ● — What difference is it making on the ground, over time?
11. Cost & Financial Sustainability ● — Is the pricing model viable and sustainable long-term?
12. Data Governance & Sovereignty ● — How are data ownership, privacy, and security handled?
13. Explainability & Human Steering — Can users understand and control why the AI gives an answer?
14. Cultural & Safety Alignment — Are outputs culturally accurate, ethically aligned, and free of regional bias?

Why some pages leave four criteria unscored

Four of the fourteen can only be answered by people using the tool in real work: 4 User Reach & Adoption, 6 Project Implementation, 9 Post-Implementation Support and 10 Retention & Sustained Impact. Where we could reach such users, a tool is assessed against all fourteen — what we call Tier 1. Where we could not, it is assessed against the other ten only, which we call Tier 2, and those four are left deliberately unscored rather than guessed at.

A Tier 2 page is not a worse tool or a lesser assessment. It usually means the tool is new, or that its users were beyond our reach in the time we had. Newer tools cannot show a user base until someone adopts them, so treating the absence of one as a mark against a tool would make it impossible for any new tool to be adopted at all. Where a tool is at Tier 2, the page says what we would need in order to answer the remaining four.

Please read this before using the scores. Every score in this document was agreed by our reviewers, who scored independently and then settled their scores together on the record, by discussion rather than by averaging or a vote. The evidence behind every score — the surveys, interviews, correspondence with builders, trial records and our own testing — is the work of our team, and each page says where every finding came from. Please treat the numbers as a starting point for your own judgement, not as settled findings.

Scripture Forge

SILscriptureforge.orgFree, open source (MIT)Web, linked to ParatextUses AI to draft

Executive summary

Scripture Forge generates a first-pass draft of a Bible book in a team's own language, using the team's completed work as the model, and delivers it into their Paratext project. It is used in several ways: drafting New Testament books, drafting incrementally as a translation grows, back translation, adaptation projects, and community checking. Its most common use is drafting the Old Testament once a team's New Testament is finished, and that is the use this assessment mostly reflects: twelve of the fifteen teams we heard from had a completed New Testament and were drafting the Old.

This assessment draws on written responses from fifteen people across seven organisations, gathered between 18 and 28 September 2026 and covering nineteen named language projects in India and Nepal, with three respondents who did not name theirs; a reading of Scripture Forge's published terms of use and privacy policy; and figures supplied by SIL. Thirteen of the fifteen are still using it.

Most teams say it solved the problem they came with, and ten have been using it for over a year. Two things temper that. Drafts are rewritten heavily — most teams keep between one and eight verses in ten with only small changes, and four teams say drafting is now slower than it was before. And nine of the fifteen have seen wording carried over from the source that does not suit their language, which for one team is a structural mismatch between an Indo-Aryan source and a Dravidian target that they correct by hand, book after book.

The main open question is what happens to the text a team sends for drafting. The terms of use predate the AI feature entirely and say nothing about it, so only SIL can say where that text is processed and whether it is used to improve their models.

Findings & Evidence

1Problem AlignmentDecisive3agreedUser-reportedTeams came to it for different reasons: several wanted drafting to go faster, one had no experienced translator available, two wanted more natural language and better key terms, one wanted Old Testament drafts generated from a New Testament already finished, and one wanted to speed up back translation, which had been holding up their targets. Twelve of the fifteen said it solved the problem they came with, one said partly, and two said it did not. On speed the picture is mixed and has become more so: three find drafting much faster, seven somewhat faster, one about the same, and four slower. The problem is real and the tool addresses it for most teams, but a quarter of them are working more slowly than before.
2Setup Complexity3agreedUser-reportedSomeone other than the team did the setup in eleven of the fifteen cases — their own organisation's support staff in seven, a partner's in one, the Scripture Forge team in two, and a named individual in one. Four teams had a member with administrator rights do it themselves. From deciding to use it to a first usable draft took under a week for four teams, one to four weeks for three, and one to three months for four; four could not say, mostly because someone else did the work. One team lost time to an incorrect language code in Paratext, and another to markers in their Paratext project.
3Workflow Integration3agreedUser-reportedEleven of the fifteen chose direct sync into Paratext. Most of the rest copy and paste, and one mostly uses direct sync but into a separate AI project, mainly for books already translated by hand, so that the AI draft can be compared and improved. The friction teams named is specific and recurring: one re-uploads a spreadsheet of proper names with every draft request and must delete the previous one first; one repeats work on some passages; one runs the generation command many times; and one describes converting the AI draft for each book as a step of its own.
4User Reach & AdoptionNeeds real users3agreedUser-reportedFifteen people answered, from seven organisations, covering nineteen named language projects — one respondent alone speaks for seven — and three who did not name theirs. Most are in India; at least one is in Nepal. That is a substantial spread of teams and roles for a tool of this kind. Against it, the builder counts 274 projects in India using Scripture Forge, some of them back translations, with 234 people holding roles on them; many hold roles on several projects. The builder also gave a lower figure, 121, for projects actively using it, and the difference between the two was not explained. Neither figure says how many teams are active now.
5Ease of Use & Learning Curve3agreedUser-reportedAsked how much training they needed for day-to-day use, one team said none, six a short walkthrough, four several sessions, and four ongoing help from someone technical — so a little over half needed sustained support rather than a one-off introduction. The obstacles named were the team's willingness to use it, an initial language-code problem, marker problems in Paratext, slow internet in the field, and the labour of correcting word order by hand where the source language and the target language differ in structure. Four teams reported no obstacle.
6Project ImplementationNeeds real users4agreedUser-reportedNineteen named language projects across seven organisations use it, and three respondents did not name theirs: eleven of the fifteen respondents use it regularly, two occasionally, and two have stopped. Ten have been using it for more than a year, and seven have drafted more than ten books each. That is comfortably past the top of the scale, which asks for five or more projects actively relying on the tool.
7Implementation Horizon4agreedDocumentationThe core features work today and are in daily use across many projects, and the tool is actively maintained. We have not found a published roadmap with dates, which is what the top of the scale asks for.
8Workflow Stage Fit3agreedUser-reportedAll fifteen teams use it for drafting, seven also generate back-translation drafts, and two use it for community checking or review. None edits inside Scripture Forge itself. Because drafts sync directly into Paratext for most teams, the drafting stage hands over cleanly to the team's own working project. The checking stages exist but are barely used, so continuity beyond drafting is still not demonstrated.
9Post-Implementation SupportNeeds real users3agreedUser-reportedSupport reaches people through several routes and generally works. Teams described contacting their own organisation's IT staff and getting a fix within a day; the Scripture Forge team by email, without much delay; and named individuals directly, including over WhatsApp. One issue took two days to resolve because it had to be handled remotely. Two respondents had nothing to report.
10Retention & Sustained ImpactNeeds real users3agreedUser-reportedThirteen of the fifteen are still using it, eleven of them regularly, and ten have been using it for over a year. Asked how much of an AI draft survives with only small changes, two teams keep one or two verses in ten, five keep three to five, four keep six to eight, one keeps none, and one could not say. Two teams have stopped, both within three months, and both gave the same reason: the draft quality was not worth the editing. One of those respondents added that the team's own willingness played a part; the other said quality was low for one of their two languages. Usage is sustained and drafts are heavily rewritten, and because we reached teams through the people who support them, teams who quietly gave up are still likely to be under-represented.
11Cost & Financial SustainabilityDecisive4agreedDocumentationIf Scripture Forge disappeared in two years, a team would lose its drafting head start and go back to drafting by hand — but nothing already produced would be lost, because every draft lands in the team's own Paratext project and stays there. Teams are not locked in. The tool is free and open source, so the code would survive its funder; what would not survive is the service that generates the drafts, which costs its builder money every time a team uses it, and which no team pays for. No end date has been published, and none has been suggested to us.
12Data Governance & SovereigntyDecisive2agreedDocumentationWe read the terms of use and the privacy policy on 23 September 2026. Both are SIL's general Language Software policies rather than anything specific to Scripture Forge, and both state that they were last updated on 30 September 2019; neither mentions AI, models, training or draft generation, so a team accepting them is not agreeing to anything specific about AI drafting. Ownership is clear: sharing data does not change who owns it. But uploading grants SIL a wide licence to host, copy, modify, make derivative works from, publish and distribute the text, for as long as it is hosted there. Translation text counts as Content rather than personal information, so the protections the privacy policy describes for personal data do not apply to it, and data from different users and services may be combined. Nothing is said about how long text is kept, where it is processed, or how to delete it from Scripture Forge, and no regional privacy law is mentioned. Disputes are handled in Texas, and an organisation using the service accepts the terms on behalf of its teams. Among the users we heard from, eight know where their text is stored and who can reach it, four know roughly, and two do not know.
13Explainability & Human Steering2agreedUser-reportedAsked whether they can tell why a draft chose a particular wording, three said usually, seven sometimes, one never, and four did not answer. Drafts arrive without any explanation attached. The way teams steer the output is indirect: feeding corrected text back in so later books improve, supplying a spreadsheet of proper names, or changing which translation is used as the source.
14Cultural & Safety Alignment2agreedUser-reportedNine of the fifteen had seen wording or ideas carried over from the source that did not fit their language or culture; three had not noticed any, two said no, and one did not answer. The examples are specific: a dragon rendered as pigs; borrowed words appearing where a concept had no precedent in the data; one team's back translation of Isaiah 34:15, which the respondent called “funny” — the wild goats calling their friends' goats; and vocabulary drawn from books the team had not yet translated. One team described the underlying problem structurally — their source text is an Indo-Aryan language and their target is Dravidian, so word order and clause order come out wrong and have to be corrected by hand, and they cannot substitute a Dravidian source text because it would diverge from the regional Bible their community expects. These look like limits of the material the model has to work from rather than a systematic bias.

Still open

  • What happens to text sent for drafting: whether it trains or improves models, where it is processed, how long it is kept. The terms of use and privacy policy, both dated 30 September 2019, predate the feature and are silent on it. Only the builder can answer.
  • How many of the 234 people with a role on these projects have used it recently, and how many projects are active now.
  • Why four teams find drafting slower while still choosing to use it, and what separates them from the teams who find it much faster.
  • Whether the teams who stopped are typical of others we did not reach. Both stopped teams came to us through the people who support them, as everyone in this survey did.
  • How much of a draft actually survives, measured rather than estimated — by comparing drafts with the finished text for a few teams.

Where to find it

scriptureforge.org · help and release notes at help.scriptureforge.org · terms at scriptureforge.org/terms and privacy at scriptureforge.org/privacy · generating a draft needs a Paratext project with roughly a complete New Testament across a range of genres.

The user it assumes

NT complete and OT in progress; Paratext linked; a technically capable member or support staff; reliable internet; source texts licensed for training; someone with Paratext administrator rights; a workflow that accepts post-editing a draft zero; and an organisation willing to accept SIL's generic language-software terms — under Texas jurisdiction, with the organisation indemnifying SIL — on behalf of its teams.
